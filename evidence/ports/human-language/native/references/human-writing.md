# Human writing and AI slop

Use with [writing rules and output checks](writing-rules.md) for voice rewrites, requests to avoid AI slop, and writing reviews. Sources were checked on September 15, 2026. The linked writing rules hold the required rules; this file explains how to judge harder cases.

## Define the aim

For this skill, good human-style writing means useful, truthful prose that fits its reader, purpose, and author. It has a clear point, enough real detail, connected ideas, and a voice suited to the situation. These are quality goals. They do not establish who wrote the text.

“AI slop” has no single settled scientific test. Use the term here for generated prose with little value: vague, wrong, repetitive, padded, poorly connected, or out of place. A smooth surface can hide those faults. Human writing can have them too. AI-assisted writing can be useful and well made.

No checklist can describe everything people write. A poem may use fragments; a contract may repeat a defined term; a kind note may use a familiar phrase. Judge each choice by its job. Simple prose should keep depth, voice, and necessary precision.

## Check the substance and the voice

| Look for | A fault to fix | Better test or repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A real point | Fluent words that do not answer the request | State what the reader learns or can do. If you cannot, rebuild the passage. |
| Relevant detail | Generic benefits, vague nouns, invented examples passed off as fact | Use supported names, actions, limits, and examples. Mark a made-up example as one. |
| Enough information | Extra words with little value, or brevity that hides a key condition | Keep what changes meaning or action. Cut what adds neither understanding nor needed tone. |
| Truth and fair support | Made-up facts, sources, certainty, motives, or sweeping claims | Tie claims to evidence. Keep doubt and disagreement where they exist. |
| Connected ideas | Nice sentences with a missing step between them | Check why each sentence follows. Name unclear references and explain needed links. |
| The author's point of view | A clear opinion softened into a stock balanced answer | Preserve the actual stance and strength. Do not add an opinion or change it to please the reader. |
| A fitting voice | The same cheerful, formal, or sales-like tone in every setting | Use the supplied voice and the reader's situation. Change tone when the event changes. |
| Natural flow | Repeated openings, filler transitions, forced symmetry, or choppy fragments | Let the logic set sentence length and structure. Read the passage aloud or review its spoken flow. |
| Purposeful structure | Lists, bold text, headings, or summaries that add work | Keep them when they help scanning, order, or comparison. Use prose when ideas depend on each other. |
| Specific care | Empty sympathy, forced humor, praise, or false closeness | Acknowledge the known effect and offer a valid action. Do not assume a feeling or shared past. |
| Respect for the reader | Childish tone, culture-bound slang, or erased dialect | Use plain adult language. Follow stated language needs and the author's chosen voice. |
| Original thought where needed | A familiar template takes the place of an explanation or creative idea | Develop the requested point from the brief. Novelty is useful in creative work, not in exact labels or stable terms. |

These tests combine several sources with practical editing judgment. They are not a copy of a validated scale or a claim that every row is independent.

## Give the prose a fitting rhythm

Rhythm is how the words move as someone reads them. It includes length, stress, pauses, and repeated forms. Use variety with a purpose. A calm notice may need a steady beat; a story may need changes in pace. Keep the grade-6 aim and the author's meaning.

| Choice | Use it when it helps | Keep this limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentence length | Mix short statements with longer linked thoughts. A brief sentence can stress a key point. | No fixed ratio, minimum spread, or short-long pattern. Never pad for variety. |
| Sentence openings | Change a repeated opening when it sounds dull. | Keep a clear subject. Repeating a key term may help more than a new opening. |
| Clause shape | Join closely linked thoughts; split a sentence that asks the reader to hold too much at once. | Keep “if,” “unless,” and “only” beside what they limit. Avoid nested side notes. |
| Emphasis | Give the key fact a clear place, often at the end of a sentence. | Lead the whole answer with what the reader needs. Do not hide an urgent action or warning for dramatic effect. |
| Pauses | Use punctuation to show how parts relate and where a thought ends. | Keep grammar, house style, and exact text intact. Extra punctuation is not better rhythm. |
| Paragraph pace | Start a new paragraph when the idea or task changes. Let a dense point have room. | Do not force equal blocks or make every sentence its own paragraph. |
| Known to new | Start from a fact the reader knows, then add the next point. | Do not repeat a whole explanation. Check that “this” and “it” have clear meanings. |
| Transitions | Show the real link: cause, contrast, example, or next step. | “So” and “therefore” need a real link. A transition word cannot repair missing logic. |
| Useful repetition | Repeat a key term for clarity; use matching grammar for equal steps. | Cut repeated claims. Keep purposeful rhythm in supplied prose, verse, and speeches. |
| Sound and register | Use words that feel natural together in the chosen voice. | Do not add rhyme, slang, alliteration, or ornate synonyms just to seem human. |

Review a paragraph in one pass for flow, then in the whole piece for pace. Read it aloud or use a supported speech tool when useful; otherwise do a silent flow review. Never claim an audio test that did not run. A stumble can reveal a hard clause, vague link, or repeated beat. Fix the cause and reread. A short UI label needs clarity, not forced variety.

These are supported editing practices, not proof that greater variation always improves understanding. Keep a good repeated form when it does useful work. Do not optimize “burstiness,” unpredictability, or any detector score.

## What research changes in this skill

| Source and kind | Finding used | Rule and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Shaib et al., Measuring AI “Slop” in Text](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v1), 2025 preprint | Expert input and annotations of news and question answers link quality judgments to several traits, including relevance, coherence, repetition, and tone. Binary judgments vary; model judges miss faults. | Review separate dimensions and cite the actual faulty span. A word list or one model verdict cannot certify quality. Results cover the study's texts and raters. |
| [Reinhart et al., Do LLMs write like humans?](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2), research article | Matched prompts across genres show grammatical and rhetorical differences. Instruction-tuned models often favor dense informational styles and adapt less to genres than people do. | Check genre fit and noun-heavy prose. Do not ban a grammar form or claim every current model behaves alike. |
| [Abdulhai et al., How LLMs Distort Our Written Language](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161v1), 2026 preprint | A writing study and separate editing tests show shifts in meaning and stance, even with narrow editing requests. | Compare meaning and viewpoint before and after. The experiments do not prove all AI edits distort meaning; the exact skill still needs tests. |
| [Can You Make It Sound Like You?](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24444v1), 2026 preprint | In the 81-person analysis, post-editing improved personal style but retained some model style by the study's measures. Writers' self-reports and model-based style measures did not fully agree. | Start from supplied details and voice; do more than swap stock words. Short tasks, fictional personal details used for privacy, and model-based measures limit general claims. |
| [Doshi and Hauser: creativity and diversity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00506), experiment | AI ideas improved ratings of individual stories in the experiment, while stories became more alike. | Preserve useful ideas while checking sameness. This is counterevidence to “all AI writing is bad”; it does not make originality the goal for a technical notice. |
| [Pangram: signs of AI writing](https://www.pangram.com/signs-of-ai-writing), vendor corpus analysis | Lists recurring surface patterns, including stock wording, repeated rhetorical shapes, and formatting used out of place. The page says no one sign proves AI use. | Use patterns as editing clues only. Keep useful lists, punctuation, and technical symbols. Corpus rates are not universal limits or the detector's stated feature list. |
| [Pangram 4 technical report](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183v1), vendor report, July 2026 | Distinguishes generated text from AI-assisted text and reports detection results on its benchmarks. | Authorship, assistance, quality, and reading ease are separate questions. Vendor accuracy claims do not validate this skill or prove a specific passage is human-written. |
| [Pangram 4 model card](https://www.pangram.com/research/model-card/pangram-4), vendor scope notes | Designed for complete prose of at least 50 words. Short replies and some technical forms fall outside its main scope. Confidence is not a calibrated probability of a correct label. | Agent output often includes those forms. Do not use detector results as a universal gate or quality score. |
| [Liang et al.: detector bias](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819), 2023 study | Some tested detectors misclassified non-native English writing and changed judgments after wording changes. | Do not punish a dialect or pursue a detector score. This study did not test current Pangram; it cannot establish that product's present accuracy. |
| [Mailchimp: voice and tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/), editorial practice | Voice can stay steady while tone changes with the reader's situation. Clarity comes before entertainment. | Keep the author's voice; adapt tone to the event. Mailchimp's humor is a brand choice, not a universal human trait. |
| [Nielsen Norman Group: tone dimensions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/), UX practice | Tone varies in formality, humor, respect, and enthusiasm. Audience and context guide the choice. | Test different genres and situations. Do not make casual speech the only passing style. |
| [Purdue OWL: concision](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/index.html), writing guidance | Effective words matter more than the smallest possible word count. Specific words and sentence edits reduce clutter. | Cut waste, not meaning. One longer precise word may beat a string of vague short ones. |
| [Google: conversation design](https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/learn-about-conversation), design guidance | Conversation relies on cooperation and context, beyond literal isolated sentences. | Resolve short replies and implied references from the exchange. This is design guidance, not proof of universal agent behavior. |
| [Purdue OWL: sentence variety](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html), writing practice | Length, openings, and sentence form can change rhythm and emphasis. | Use purposeful variety. This guidance does not test an ideal ratio or prove human authorship. |
| [UNC: flow](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/flow/), writing practice | Clear references, known-to-new order, nearby subjects and verbs, and parallel forms support flow. | Repeated structure can help. Do not treat all repetition as slop. |
| [Harvard: transitions](https://writingcenter.fas.harvard.edu/transitions), writing practice | Links between ideas matter more than adding transition words. | Check the logic first, including whether a claimed cause is real. Academic examples need adaptation to other genres. |
| [Gopen and Swan: The Science of Scientific Writing](https://inpp.ohio.edu/~meisel/PHYS6751/file/ScientificWriting_GGopenJSwanAmSci1990.pdf), 1990 explanatory essay, university-hosted copy | Sentence placement affects context and emphasis; structure and meaning interact. | Treat this as a reader-expectation framework, not a controlled test of this skill. Preserve intent when moving words. |
| [UNC: reading aloud](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/reading-aloud/), revision practice | Hearing prose can expose faults missed during silent reading. | Useful for review; it does not prove comprehension. Silent review is distinct from an actual audio test. |
| [Morkes and Nielsen: writing for the web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/), 1997 task study | Concise, scannable, less promotional versions helped users on the tested site. | An older, small, single-site study is not a universal effect estimate. Its useful lists are counterevidence to blanket formatting bans. |
| [GOV.UK: clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/), editorial practice | Use the audience's words and explain needed specialist terms. | Local house rules do not define all natural prose. |

For audience testing, accessibility, reading levels, and agent context, see [research and limits](research.md). Its Digital.gov, CDC, W3C, CMS, and agent-system sources complement the writing studies above.

## Diagnose and repair, without tricks

1. Find the exact passage and name what fails: meaning, truth, relevance, flow, voice, or fit.
2. Identify its cause. A vague brief may need a fact; a bloated sentence may need a direct verb; a stock answer may need a fresh outline.
3. Make the smallest complete repair. Do not just replace a flagged word with another grand word.
4. Read the whole piece again. Check what the edit changed in nearby sentences and the author's stance.
5. Run the required [output checks](writing-rules.md#check-before-sending-or-saving). Keep a failure open until the revised text passes.

**Weak:** “We are committed to providing a seamless experience as we navigate this issue.”

**Known facts:** The export is delayed. No cause or finish time has been confirmed.

**Useful:** “Your export is delayed. We don't yet know when it will be ready.” Add a next step only if one is valid and known.

**False improvement:** “I've been there, and I know how frustrating this is.” This invents experience and assumes the reader's feelings.

**Another false improvement:** “Your export will be ready soon.” Short, warm wording does not make the unsupported promise true.

## Evidence standard

Keep source findings, vendor claims, editorial advice, and this skill's design choices distinct. Do not claim that surface cues establish authorship, that removing them proves quality, or that tests cover all people, genres, languages, and future models. Use actual reader feedback when claiming comprehension or voice fit with people. Model tests are useful evidence with a narrower scope.
