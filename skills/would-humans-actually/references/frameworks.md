# Diagnostic frameworks

Apply these only after defining `[x]` and collecting direct or close evidence. They organize questions. They do not supply evidence or probabilities.

## Behavior specification

| Element     | Question                                                          |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Actor       | Who performs the action?                                          |
| Action      | What could an observer record?                                    |
| Setting     | Where, through which channel, and after what trigger?             |
| Time        | By when, how often, and for how long?                             |
| Cost        | What money, effort, privacy, status, or opportunity cost is paid? |
| Alternative | What happens instead?                                             |
| Completion  | What event counts, and what does not?                             |

## Motivation, ability, and prompt

Ask whether the desired outcome matters at action time, what immediate cost or competing reward opposes it, whether time and resources permit it, whether the next action is clear, and whether the cue arrives when action is possible. Do not multiply these factors into a probability or assume one always dominates.

## Capability, opportunity, and motivation

Check physical and psychological capability, physical and social opportunity, reflective plans and beliefs, and automatic emotion, impulse, or habit. Liking the idea is not the same as having the capability and opportunity to act.

## Deliberate action

Inspect expected benefits and costs, perceived norms, perceived and actual control, and whether a concrete intention exists. This view is less informative for action driven mainly by habit, impulse, emotion, or constraints the person misperceives.

## Habit and repetition

Keep frequency, stable context, automaticity, and persistence separate. Ask which cue starts the current behavior, whether `[x]` competes with an established routine, whether repetition occurs in one context, whether reward is close to the action, and which retention window distinguishes routine from a short burst. No universal habit timeline applies.

## Friction and reward timing

Map time, money, effort, risk, social cost, identity cost, and opportunity cost at the moment of action. Map each reward as immediate or delayed, certain or uncertain, private or visible, and one-time or recurring. Treat every proposed bottleneck as a hypothesis to test.

## Social and cultural fit

Ask who can observe `[x]`, which group sets the relevant norm, what approval or sanction follows, whether the action changes meaning across roles or cultures, and whether the evidence population shares the target incentives and constraints. Do not generalize one student, online, national, or channel sample to everyone.

## Switching and reversal

Inspect sunk learning, setup, stored data, migration work, coordination, reliability risk, policy limits, reversibility, and loss of status or control. A real switch may require more value than a trial click, but no fixed multiplier is valid without target data.

## Outside-view fit

A useful reference class has the same outcome, a similar population and setting, comparable costs and alternatives, a matching window, explicit inclusion rules, enough cases for a base rate, and a reason target evidence should update it. Report sensitivity when another plausible class changes the answer.
